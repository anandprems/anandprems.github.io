The Most Important Output of a Classroom Is Not Marks

This thought came to me while taking classes for first-year and fourth-year students at the same time.

Two groups of students. Two completely different stages of college life. But when I look at both groups, I feel that there is one common thing we often forget.

Marks are important. But marks should not become the only purpose of learning.

Yes, marks matter. They are part of academic life. They help students progress through their courses, apply for opportunities, and build confidence.

But there is a difference between working for marks and begging for marks.

We should work for knowledge. We should work for skills. And let the marks follow.

Don't Start With "How Many Marks?"

Whenever a new subject begins, the first question should not always be: "How many marks will this topic carry?"

Instead, ask: "Where can I use this knowledge?"

Maybe the subject is difficult. Maybe it doesn't immediately look interesting. Maybe it is not something you would choose yourself. And that is completely okay. Not every subject will be everyone's cup of tea.

But before deciding that a subject is useless or boring, spend some time understanding it. What problem does it solve? Where is it used in the real world? What skills can I develop from it? Where does this subject connect with technology, industry, research or my future career?

Once we understand the purpose, learning becomes different. We start learning with a reason.

Don't Stop With the Syllabus

A curriculum gives us a direction. But it should not become a boundary.

The syllabus may tell you what to learn. Your curiosity should tell you what to explore next.

Complete the syllabus. Understand the fundamentals. But then ask: "What else is there?"

Search. Read. Watch. Experiment. Discuss with your teachers. Try something on your own. Make mistakes. Try again.

That is where real learning starts.

Build Your Own Roadmap

Once you understand the subject, create a simple roadmap. Don't try to learn everything in one day. Take it one step at a time.

Learn → Practice → Apply → Explore

Touch every important concept first. Then go deeper into the areas that interest you. And most importantly, use what you learn — because knowledge becomes much stronger when you apply it.

Turn Knowledge Into Something

This is where I would love to see more students move. Don't let your learning remain only inside your notebook.

Take one concept. Build a mini project. Then improve it. Turn it into a bigger project. Then ask: "Can I solve a real problem with this?"

That question can take you much further. A project can lead to an experiment. An experiment can lead to an observation. An observation can lead to a small research idea. That research idea can become a conference-level student paper. And if the work is strong enough, why not explore it further with your faculty and work towards a journal-level research publication?

There is no guarantee that every project will reach that stage. And that is not the point. The point is the journey.

Learn → Mini Project → Project → Research Idea → Conference → Journal

One step at a time.

And Then, Share What You Learn

Today, learning does not have to end when the project is submitted. You can share your journey.

Write about what you learned on LinkedIn. Create a small explanation video on YouTube. Share your project. Explain your mistakes. Show your experiments. Talk about what worked and what didn't.

When you share your work, something interesting happens. People respond. Someone may ask a question. Someone may suggest another approach. Someone from another college may connect with you. A researcher may notice your work. A professional may give you an idea.

And suddenly, your small classroom project becomes the beginning of a much bigger learning journey.

Learning creates skills. Skills create work. Work creates opportunities. Sharing creates connections. Connections create more learning.

So, What Should a Student Really Aim For?

Not just: "How do I get 90 marks?"

But: "How do I understand this subject?" Then: "How can I apply it?" Then: "What can I build?" Then: "What problem can I solve?" Then: "Can I explore something new?" And finally: "Can I share what I learned and learn from others?"

If we follow that path sincerely, marks will still matter. But they won't be something we have to constantly chase. They can become a natural outcome of understanding the subject well.

From First Year to Final Year

This is perhaps what I see most clearly when I teach first-year and fourth-year students simultaneously.

A first-year student is just beginning to discover the world of engineering. A fourth-year student is standing close to the professional world. The journey between those two stages is not just about completing semesters. It is about transformation.

From learning concepts to building skills. From following instructions to solving problems. From doing assignments to building projects. From building projects to asking questions. From asking questions to doing research.

And eventually, from being a student who consumes knowledge to becoming someone who creates and shares knowledge.

That is the journey I hope more students will take.

One Last Thought

Marks are important. Let's not pretend they aren't. But if marks become the destination, we may miss the journey.

Learn the subject. Understand why it matters. Explore beyond the syllabus. Build something. Make mistakes. Improve it. Try a bigger problem. Explore research. Share your work. Learn from people around you.

And somewhere along that journey, you may realize: you were not studying just to get marks. You were preparing yourself for something much bigger.

And perhaps, the most important output of a classroom was never the mark sheet. It was the mind that learned how to think.
